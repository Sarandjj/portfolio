> **Short answer:** know your one-sentence goal, who will use it and on which phones, the three screens it cannot live without, and who decides. Create the store and domain accounts in your own name. A developer can do everything else, but only you can supply these.

If you are about to hire someone to build an app, most delays and surprise costs do not come from the code. They come from decisions that were not made yet. These seven are the ones I would sort out first, from shipping apps to both the Google Play Store and the Apple App Store.

## 1. One sentence that says what the app is for

Not a feature list. A sentence such as "Parents see their child's attendance the same day" or "Players book a court in three taps". If you cannot say it in one sentence, the app will try to do everything and do none of it well. Every feature later gets the same test: does it help that sentence?

## 2. Who uses it, and on what phone

Name the person: a parent, a driver, a gym member. Then find out what they actually hold. In many places most users are on Android phones that are a few years old, on slow connections. That changes real decisions: how big the app can be, whether it must work offline, and which languages it needs.

## 3. The three screens it cannot live without

Write down the three screens (or actions) a user must be able to do on day one. Everything else is "version two". A small first release gets into people's hands, and what they tell you after using it is worth more than a long plan.

## 4. Accounts you should own, in your name

This is the one people most often get wrong. Create these yourself and add the developer as a team member, rather than the developer creating them for you:

- A Google Play developer account (a one-time registration fee).
- An Apple Developer Program membership (a yearly fee), which you need for the App Store.
- The domain name for the app's website and privacy policy.
- Any service the app depends on, such as the Firebase or Supabase project and the payment gateway account.

If the developer owns the store account, you do not fully own your app, and moving it later is painful.

## 5. The words, images and languages

Logo, app name, short description, screenshots and the text inside the app. If your users read Tamil, Hindi or another language, decide now whether the app should have it from day one. Adding a language later touches every screen, and long words in some languages break layouts that were only tested in English.

## 6. Money and legal basics

- If people will pay inside the app, you need a payment gateway account, and that takes time to verify.
- Both stores ask for a privacy policy link. If the app collects any user data, you need to know what and why.
- If your users are children or students, be extra careful about what data you keep and who can see it.

## 7. Who decides, and how you will review

Pick one person who can say yes or no. Agree on a short review rhythm, for example a working build to try every week or two. Teams that review small pieces often find problems while they are still cheap to fix.

## A quick checklist

- [ ] One-sentence goal
- [ ] Who uses it, and on what phones
- [ ] Three must-have screens
- [ ] Play Store, Apple and domain accounts in your name
- [ ] Logo, texts, languages
- [ ] Payment and privacy basics
- [ ] One decision-maker and a review rhythm
